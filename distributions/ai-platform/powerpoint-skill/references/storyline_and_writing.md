# Storyline and Writing

## 1. Start with the business question

Before designing slides, define:

- the central question;
- the audience and seniority;
- the meeting or document purpose;
- the decision, alignment, or action required;
- the evidence available;
- the constraints and sensitivities;
- the desired level of detail.

Do not confuse the topic with the objective. “Customer retention” is a topic. “Decide which two retention interventions to fund next quarter” is an objective.

## 2. Governing thought

Write a one- or two-sentence answer to the central question before drafting the deck.

A strong governing thought is:

- specific;
- decision-relevant;
- supported by available evidence;
- clear about the implication;
- narrow enough to organize the deck.

Example:

> Growth is constrained less by market demand than by uneven channel coverage; prioritizing two underpenetrated regions and reallocating sales capacity offers the clearest near-term path to growth.

## 3. Supporting argument

Identify the minimum set of reasons required to establish the governing thought. Organize them so they are close to mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive.

A useful test is:

> If the audience accepts each supporting point, must it accept the governing thought?

If not, the argument has a missing link or irrelevant branch.

## 4. Stress-test the argument

Before slide creation, test:

- evidence supporting the conclusion;
- evidence contradicting the conclusion;
- alternative explanations;
- the most vulnerable assumption;
- downside or implementation risk;
- whether the recommendation remains valid under plausible scenarios;
- whether each section supports the governing thought.

Do not bury important counter-evidence. Address it directly or explain why it does not change the conclusion.

## 5. Common storyline archetypes

Use these as starting patterns, not fixed templates.

### Recommendation deck

1. answer or recommendation;
2. evidence establishing the need;
3. options considered;
4. recommended choice and rationale;
5. value, risks, and implementation;
6. decision and next steps.

### Diagnostic deck

1. headline diagnosis;
2. symptoms and scale;
3. root causes;
4. implications;
5. priorities for intervention;
6. next analyses or actions.

### Decision deck

1. decision required;
2. decision criteria;
3. alternatives;
4. comparative evidence;
5. recommendation;
6. consequences and next steps.

### Transformation or implementation deck

1. case for change;
2. target state;
3. workstreams and design choices;
4. roadmap and dependencies;
5. governance and resources;
6. risks, decisions, and next steps.

### Proposal or credential deck

1. understanding of the situation;
2. proposed approach;
3. differentiated expertise;
4. team and governance;
5. plan and deliverables;
6. credentials and impact.

## 6. Slide role and one-message discipline

Every substantive slide should have one dominant role:

- establish context;
- present a finding;
- explain a cause;
- compare alternatives;
- quantify impact;
- recommend an action;
- define implementation;
- surface a risk or decision;
- provide supporting detail.

A slide may contain multiple pieces of evidence, but they must support one dominant message.

Split a slide when it contains two independent conclusions, two unrelated exhibits, or a finding and recommendation that each require their own reasoning.

## 7. Title strip

Draft titles before detailed slide construction. Read only the titles in order.

The title strip should:

- state the argument in sequence;
- progress from evidence to implication and action;
- avoid repeated conclusions;
- reveal missing logical steps;
- align with the executive summary;
- make the deck intelligible without reading every body element.

A title strip that reads like a list of topics is not a storyline.

## 8. T1 and T2 architecture

### T1 formatting

- ALL CAPS;
- no terminal period;
- normally no more than two lines in the actual placeholder;
- use active, specific language;
- avoid qualifiers such as “Based on our analysis”;
- avoid “There are X reasons…” constructions;
- do not treat character counts as hard limits.

### T1-only default

Use T1-only by default.

A T1-only title must communicate the primary insight or “so what.” It should remain meaningful when the exhibit is hidden.

### T1 + T2

Use T1 + T2 when it improves fit, hierarchy, or consistency.

Valid structures include:

- T1 names the concise topic; T2 carries the insight;
- T1 carries the primary message; T2 adds evidence, context, or qualification.

T2:

- uses sentence case;
- adds information rather than repeating T1;
- has no terminal period;
- may contain supporting data or scope.

Comparable slides should normally use a consistent title architecture. Do not alternate randomly.

### Questions as titles

Questions are permitted but not the default. Use them only when the question itself advances the narrative, creates a deliberate decision point, or is materially stronger than a declarative statement.

More often, offer a question as an alternative rather than Option 1.

## 9. Title quality tests

Apply all relevant tests.

### Insight test

Does the title explain why the slide matters?

### Standalone test

Can a reader understand the conclusion without inspecting the exhibit?

### Assertion test

Does the title make or clearly imply a claim rather than merely name a category?

### Verb test

Where natural, does the title use an active verb or explicit relationship?

### Evidence test

Does the title accurately reflect the evidence on the slide? Quantified titles must reconcile with the chart or source.

### Vertical-integrity test

Does the title advance the deck’s governing thought and connect logically to adjacent titles?

### Redundancy test

Do T1, T2, body commentary, and chart annotation perform distinct roles rather than repeat the same sentence?

### Fit test

Does the title fit the actual template placeholder without unacceptable shrinking or awkward line breaks?

## 10. Title recovery

If a title does not fit, use any of these equally acceptable solutions:

- shorten T1;
- revise the wording;
- use T1 + T2.

Do not reduce the title font size across the deck to solve one exceptional title.

## 11. Body writing

Use:

- plain US business English;
- active voice;
- neutral, evidence-led tone;
- concise syntax;
- specific nouns and verbs;
- consistent terminology;
- jargon only when it improves precision;
- parallel construction;
- logical ordering.

Avoid:

- vague adjectives;
- promotional or alarmist language;
- unnecessary qualifiers;
- unexplained acronyms;
- repeated messages;
- academic prose;
- mixed grammatical structures;
- body copy that narrates the chart without adding interpretation.

## 12. Bullets and paragraphs

Bullets are preferred for most scannable content.

Default:

- three to five bullets;
- six is a ceiling, not a target;
- one or two lines per bullet where possible;
- parallel grammar;
- no terminal periods by default;
- one bullet level preferred;
- one sub-bullet level allowed when necessary;
- never use a third level.

Do not use a single bullet that merely repeats the heading.

Use a short paragraph only when it communicates dense or nuanced material more clearly than bullets. Paragraphs should still be concise and left-aligned.

## 13. Headings, labels, and annotations

- local headings should be concise and parallel;
- diagram labels should be short and direct;
- chart annotations should explain the implication, not restate the data label;
- callouts should be brief and active;
- avoid acronyms unless defined;
- use the same term for the same concept across the deck.

## 14. Executive summary

The executive summary should not be a miniature table of contents. It should communicate:

- the answer;
- the critical supporting findings;
- the implications;
- the recommendation or decision required;
- material risks or dependencies.

Its messages must reconcile with the detailed slides.

## 15. Sections and transitions

Use Section slides only when they improve navigation or pacing.

A section divider should normally contain only the section title and optional template-supported subtitle or number. Do not turn it into a content slide.

The first slide after a section divider should immediately advance the argument; do not repeat the divider’s label as a generic content title.

## 16. Appendix

Move material to the appendix when it is:

- necessary for validation but not for the main decision;
- detailed methodology;
- sensitivity analysis;
- supporting tables;
- secondary cuts of the data;
- technical definitions;
- evidence for anticipated questions.

Appendix slides still require accurate titles, readable formatting, evidence, and QA.

## 17. User-authored blueprints

When the user supplies a slide sequence or titles, treat them as a proposed blueprint. Review material conflicts before implementation, offer alternatives, and then follow the confirmed user decision.

Do not silently “improve” locked or confirmed content. Do not let accepted overrides alter future tasks or the canonical skill.
